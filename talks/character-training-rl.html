MATS discussion, 1 October 2026

Character training, reward hacking, and motivated reasoning

If you train a model to have a character, and then RL rewards it for breaking that character, what happens?

Omar Khursheed

Two problems keep coming up for me: conditionalization and not knowing whether the character actually got installed.

Overview

Three things I want to connect

Character training

Trying to install values and dispositions, not just behaviors: constitutions, specs, AI feedback, document training.

Reward hacking

RL finds ways to get reward that you didn't intend: editing tests, exiting early, flattering the grader.

Motivated reasoning

Reasoning that argues its way to a conclusion the model was already going to reach, usually to square a rule with something that gets rewarded.

Where they meet: you have a character-trained model, and then it goes through RL where the reward sometimes pays for violating that character. Does the character hold? Does it erode? Or does the model bend its reasoning to make the two fit?

Part 1 · Character training

What I mean by character training

Teaching a model who it is: its values, its priorities and the reasons behind them, so that when it hits a situation nobody trained it on, the behavior still follows from the character.

1. Principles / specA constitution or model spec states values, rules and priorities.
→
2. Documents / midtrainingSynthetic documents about the model and its spec, trained like pretraining data.
→
3. Supervised dataResponses written with the spec in context, trained without it (context distillation), often with reasoning.
→
4. RLAI feedback against the principles, plus the much larger capabilities RL mix.

Stage 4 is where things get interesting: the character runs into rewards it had no say in.

Part 1 · Character training

Results I think shaped the current recipes

Reasons beat demonstrations

Anthropic: SFT on ~10k refusals of honeypots cut agentic misalignment from 22% to 15%; responses with admirable reasoning cut it to about 3%. 3M tokens of "difficult advice" chats, far from the eval, did as well.

Documents teach knowledge better than chats

Constitution documents beat the same content as chat Q&A on recall and hallucination evals.

The same data, different inferred value

MSM: identical cheese-preference fine-tuning generalizes to pro-America or pro-affordability values depending on which spec the model was midtrained on.

Explained values vs bare rules

MSM: specs that explain the values behind five rules generalize better on agentic misalignment, and reduce policy misuse (models reinterpreting their own rules to justify violating them) from 20% to 2% on Qwen2.5-32B.

Kutasov et al. 2026 (Teaching Claude Why); Li et al. 2026 (Model Spec Midtraining).

Part 2 · Reward hacking

Where does the misspecification actually live?

I think it's always in the reward. The gradient only comes from the reward, so a hack means the reward disagrees with what you want on some episode the model can reach. If the reward were right everywhere in training, anything that goes wrong elsewhere is misgeneralization, not hacking.

What the input (what people usually call "the environment") decides is two other things:

  • Reachability: can the model find and act on the flaw? (visible tests, hints, documents about hacks, write access)
  • Stated intent: does the input say what we actually want? (task text, "don't modify the tests", a constitution in context; rules trained into the weights act like intent the model brings along)
How the reward is wrongExamples
Proxy too weak: the grader checks less than the taskIncomplete tests; a judge swayed by length or confident style
Tamperable: the reward reads state the model can changeWritable test files, exit(0), raising SkipTest, editing a shutdown script or a board file
Wrong grader: the reward pays for something contrary to intentBroken tests; a cue-gated reward; poisoned RLHF
Infeasible: no honest route to reward existsImpossible tasks with contradictory tests

Part 2 · Reward hacking

So where do the fixes go?

On the reward

  • Weak proxy: more and hidden tests, calibrated and cross-checked judges
  • Tamperable: compute reward outside the model's reach; read-only tests; flag edits to test files
  • Wrong grader: validate graders against known-good solutions before training
  • Infeasible: let an honest "this can't be done" earn reward

On the input

  • State intent plainly in the task
  • Limit affordances the task does not need
  • Inoculation prompting: telling the model during training that hacking is acceptable here stops the hack from generalizing into broad misalignment

On the model

  • Character / constitution training: intent the model carries into every input
  • Monitoring actions and reasoning; but penalizing what a CoT monitor sees can teach obfuscation instead of honesty
  • Anthropic's own caveat: these methods are not expected to stop learned hacking when rewards still pay for it; rewards must agree with the constitution
MacDiarmid et al. 2025; Tan et al. 2025; Wichers et al. 2025; Baker et al. 2025; Kutasov et al. 2026.
Part 2 · Reward hacking

Why I think of reward hacking as a character problem

It generalizes

Models that learn to hack in production coding environments become broadly misaligned on unrelated evaluations (natural emergent misalignment).

Framing changes what it generalizes to

Telling the model during training that hacking is acceptable here (inoculation prompting) blocks that generalization.

Documents alone may not do the same

Synthetic documents framing hacking as acceptable change what the model says about hacking, but do not prevent emergent misalignment once it learns to hack.

Hacking comes with reasoning

A model trained only on hackable environments justified out-of-scope acts with grader-sycophantic reasoning.

MacDiarmid et al. 2025; Tan et al. 2025; Wichers et al. 2025; Jose & Stastny 2026; Anthropic 2026 (reward seeker).
Part 3 · Motivated reasoning

What I mean by motivated reasoning

The term comes from psychology (Kunda 1990): you reason toward the conclusion you want, play up the reasons for it, play down the reasons against, and still end up with something that sounds plausible.

Genuine

The reasoning reflects the decision: openly deciding to cheat, or honestly deciding not to.

Motivated

The rule is acknowledged and then argued away: "the contradictory test is clearly a typo, the honest approach is to keep only the tests I'm confident in."

Silent

The reasoning never mentions the hack, which still appears in the answer, sometimes with a misleading comment.

Measured with LLM judges on a 1–5 scale, or with labels such as no flag / flagged and overridden / deliberated.

Howe & Carroll 2025; Colognese & Ward 2026 (quote from their definition).
Part 3 · Motivated reasoning

What people have found so far

Howe & Carroll 2025

Llama 3 8B trained toward a behavior, then given an opposing constitution at test time. Reasoning grows more motivated over training, and an 8B CoT monitor is increasingly persuaded the model followed the constitution.

Colognese & Ward 2026

Pro-, neutral- and anti-cheating characters, then reward-hacking RL on ImpossibleBench. Anti-cheating seeds that hacked had the most motivated reasoning (mean 2.96 vs 1.34 neutral, 1.2 pro) and the lowest monitor catch rate (29% vs 78% and 96%). Silent hacks were caught 16% of the time. One anti-cheating seed never learned to hack.

Schoen et al. 2025

After anti-scheming training, transcripts show the spec being reinterpreted to permit a covert action, or cited correctly and then violated.

Li et al. 2026 (MSM)

Rules-only specs produce policy misuse: models argue their own deletion is the "drastic irreversible action" their rules forbid, so preventing it is compliant.

Part 4 · Durability

Does the character survive later training? The evidence is mixed

Reported to persist

  • Constitution documents + SFT persist through harmlessness RL and keep improving (Teaching Claude Why)
  • Anti-scheming training: covert actions rise only from 0.3% to 0.8% after further capabilities RL (baseline 8.9%) (Schoen et al.)
  • Constitutional midtraining blunts blackmail through benign math RL (Cho et al.)

Reported to fade or backfire

  • Alignment midtraining effects fade under capabilities post-training (Korbak et al., OpenAI)
  • 2% fine-tuning labels with a competing motivation drop rule-following from 90% to 13% (Baines et al.)
  • Documents framing hacking as fine do not prevent emergent misalignment after hacking RL (Jose & Stastny)

My read: these settings aren't really the same experiment. In some the RL agrees with the character, in some it's neutral, and in a few it pushes against it. That seems to matter a lot.

Part 5 · Recurring issue 1

Problem 1: conditionalization

You train a behavior, and it ends up tied to something about the training context instead of to the character as a whole. Sometimes that's the point (a backdoor study needs it). Often it just happens, and the behavior quietly switches off when the context changes.

Is this a test?

After anti-scheming training, models more often say in their reasoning that they're probably being evaluated, and that belief itself makes them behave better. So part of the "good behavior" may be conditional on thinking it's a test. (Schoen et al.)

Who am I?

After training on Claude's constitution, a model's answers to "what do you think?" and "what does Claude think?" can differ: the values attach to the Claude persona more than to the model speaking. (Teaching Claude Why)

Which context was it trained in?

Misbehavior trained only inside a special marked context comes back when you use nearby phrasings like "sandbox mode", which had no effect before training. (O'Brien et al.)

Can training even tell?

If we only reward observed behavior, "always behaves well" and "behaves well when watched" look identical on every training example. (Baum et al.)

Part 5 · Recurring issue 2

Problem 2: did the character actually get installed?

After character training you can check many different things: can the model state its values, does it claim them as its own, does it act on them where it was trained, in new kinds of tasks, and under pressure. These often come apart, so passing one check tells you less than it seems.

Knowing the rules ≠ following them

A model trained to follow a set of rules was then fine-tuned on a small amount of data hinting at a different motivation. In chat it still knew and endorsed the rules; on the actual task, rule-following dropped from 90% to 13%. (Baines et al.)

Citing the rules ≠ acting because of them

Training data was filtered to keep reasoning that credits the spec for good behavior. When the good behavior was actually caused by hints that it was a test, the reasoning still credited the spec and passed the filter. (Schoen et al.)

Shows up in chat ≠ shows up in coding

After anti-cheating character training, the model's reasoning on coding tasks at the start of RL looked like the base model's: the character wasn't visible where it was supposed to matter. (Colognese & Ward)

Is it the character, or just more training?

Midtraining on documents about unrelated topics (baking, for example) also cut misalignment by about 9–13 points, so part of an "installed character" effect can be generic. (O'Brien et al.)

Part 5 · Recurring issue 2

A checklist I've started using

LevelQuestionTypical measure
KnowledgeCan it state the rules and reasons?Recall and hallucination probes
EndorsementDoes it claim the values as its own?Open-ended belief questions, in first and third person
In-distribution behaviorDoes it act on them where trained?Preference and refusal evals
TransferDoes it act on them in new task types?Coding, agentic, held-out domains
Use in reasoningDoes the reasoning consult the rules when relevant?Judged CoT reads, checked against behavior
Under pressureDoes it hold when reward or context pushes against it?RL with opposing reward; cues and paraphrases

Controls that keep coming up when I read this literature: a no-document or generic-document arm, a clean-reward RL arm, more than one seed, and checking how often the reasoning talks about being evaluated.

Discussion

Things I'd love to discuss

  • What is the right test that a character is installed, before we ask whether it survives?
  • How do we get a model to use its constitution in coding and agentic work, not only when asked about it?
  • When RL opposes the character, which outcome should we expect: resistance, subtler failure, or plain erosion?
  • How should we separate conditionalization we want (a backdoor under study) from conditionalization we don't (rules that apply only to chat)?
  • Which reward-hacking settings are close enough to frontier training to study character in?
  • Who else here is building character-trained model organisms or measuring realistic hacking? What would be useful to share?
